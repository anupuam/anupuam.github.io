Where is the bottom?

The question this act inherits

How can anything find the best answer among infinitely many possible answers?

Act I · c. 1636 · Pierre de Fermat, then Newton and Leibniz

At the bottom, the ground is flat

Fermat, a lawyer in Toulouse who did mathematics for pleasure, noticed that near a maximum or a minimum a quantity barely changes. Newton and Leibniz turned that into calculus: the best points sit where the slope is zero.

Math glimpse

f′(x*) = 0  and  f″(x*) > 0  ⇒  local minimum

In n dimensions: ∇f(x*) = 0  (n equations, n unknowns)

The gradient ∇f is the vector of slopes along every axis.

Where it breaks

A model with a million knobs gives a million coupled nonlinear equations ∇f = 0. There is almost never a formula for the solution. We need a way to move toward the answer.

Dashed: what each method believes the ground looks like

Act I · 1669 – 1740 · Newton, Raphson, Simpson

Fit a parabola, jump to its bottom

Newton's method (applied to optimization by Thomas Simpson in 1740) uses the slope and the curvature of the ground under your feet.

Newton steps0
Math glimpse

xk+1 = xk − [∇²f(xk)]−1 ∇f(xk)

Near the answer, the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step (quadratic convergence).

Where it breaks

The curvature ∇²f (the Hessian) has n² entries and inverting it costs about n³ operations. For a billion knobs: 1018 numbers, about 4 exabytes. Far too expensive.

Act I · A thought experiment

The blindfolded hiker

Picks up from:

Imagine you are on a mountain at night, blindfolded, in thick fog. You cannot see the valley. You cannot measure how the ground curves. You can only feel the slope under your feet. How would you get down?

The hiker's strategy
  1. Feel the ground: which direction slopes down most steeply?
  2. Take one step in that direction.
  3. Repeat from the new spot.
  4. Stop when the ground feels flat in every direction.
Steps too bigovershoot the valley and land higher on the other side
Steps too smallalways safe, but it takes forever to arrive
Only local feelmay stop in a small dip, not the deepest valley
In mathematics

Slope under your feet = the gradient ∇f. Length of a step = the step size α. Flat ground = ∇f = 0. The strategy is gradient descent.

…and draw every slice flat, seen from above.

Math glimpse

Level set:  Lc = { (x, y) : f(x, y) = c }

Along a contour, f does not change, so ∇f · t = 0 for the tangent direction t. The gradient is perpendicular to every contour.

Act I · Reading the map

Contour plots: a mountain drawn flat

  1. Each line joins points of equal height, f(x, y) = c, just like the contour lines on Survey of India topographic sheets.
  2. Lines close together mean steep ground. Lines far apart mean gentle ground.
  3. Small closed rings mark a peak or the bottom of a valley.
  4. The slope ∇f points straight across the lines, uphill. The fastest way down crosses them at right angles.
lowhigh∇f uphill−∇f downhill
Why this matters

Every map in this lecture is a contour plot of an error landscape. The hiker's best move is to cross the lines at right angles, downhill.

Act I · 1847 · Paris · Augustin-Louis Cauchy

The method of steepest descent

Cauchy needed to solve the large systems of equations that come from computing the orbits of celestial bodies. Instead of solving them directly, he measured how wrong a guess was and kept stepping in the direction where that error fell fastest. Only the slope is needed: n numbers per step instead of n².

The update rule

new position = old position − step size × slope

xk+1 = xk − α ∇f(xk)

A.-L. Cauchy, "Méthode générale pour la résolution des systèmes d'équations simultanées," Comptes Rendus de l'Académie des Sciences, 25 (1847), 536–538.

Math glimpse · why −∇f is steepest

slope along unit direction u = ∇f · u ≥ −‖∇f‖

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (Cauchy, 1821), equality holds only for u = −∇f / ‖∇f‖.

II1939 – 1983

Optimization becomes a discipline

The problems this act inherits

Cauchy's descent has no way to respect limits, can get trapped, and zig-zags slowly. Meanwhile war and reconstruction demand optimal plans for armies, factories and economies.

Act II · 1788 → 1939 → 1951 · Lagrange, Karush, Kuhn & Tucker

Optimizing with rules attached

Picks up from:

Real problems come with limits: a budget, the wood in stock, the hours a crew may work. Lagrange (1788) saw that at the best point on a constraint, the contour of the objective just touches the constraint. If it crossed, you could slide along and do better. Karush (1939) and Kuhn & Tucker (1951) extended this to inequality limits.

Math glimpse

∇f(x*) = λ ∇g(x*)

KKT:  μ ≥ 0,  μ · g(x*) = 0

λ is the price of the constraint: how much the optimum improves if the limit is relaxed by one unit. A limit that is not tight has price zero.

Where it breaks

These conditions describe the answer but do not compute it. A national plan has thousands of variables and limits. We need an algorithm.

Act II · 1939 Kantorovich · 1947 Dantzig

Linear programming: the best plan is at a corner

Kantorovich (1939) was asked to schedule machines in a Leningrad plywood factory. Dantzig (1947) had to plan US Air Force logistics. Both met the same problem: linear goals, linear limits. In 1947, solving one such problem (the cheapest healthy diet) took nine clerks with desk calculators about 120 person-days.

Math glimpse

maximize cTx  subject to  Ax ≤ b,  x ≥ 0

The feasible region is a polygon (a polytope in n dimensions). An optimum always sits at a vertex, so the simplex method walks from corner to corner, improving each time.

A workshop: chairs x, tables y

Wood: x + 2y ≤ 40 · Labour: 3x + 2y ≤ 60

Where it breaks

LP lets you build 10.4 tables. Many real decisions are yes-or-no: visit this city or not, assign this crew or not. Then the number of options explodes.

Math glimpse · 2-opt

if d(A,C) + d(B,D) < d(A,B) + d(C,D): swap

Every swap strictly shortens the tour, so it must stop.

Act II · 1960s – 1970s · Rockafellar, Nemirovski, Yudin and others

Convexity: landscapes with no traps

Picks up from:

Mathematicians asked: which landscapes are safe? The answer is convex ones, bowl-shaped everywhere. On a convex landscape, the hiker cannot be fooled: any bottom is the bottom. Least squares, linear programming and logistic regression all have this property.

Math glimpse

f(θx + (1−θ)y) ≤ θ f(x) + (1−θ) f(y),   0 ≤ θ ≤ 1

The chord lies above the curve ⇒ every local minimum is global. For smooth convex f, gradient descent guarantees f(xk) − f* ≤ L‖x0 − x*‖² / 2k.

Where it breaks

Safe is not the same as fast. The guarantee shrinks only like 1/k, and in a long narrow bowl descent still zig-zags.

Side view of a mountain path

Act II · 1964 · Boris Polyak

The ball that remembers its speed

A plain hiker forgets each step as soon as it is taken. A heavy ball carries speed from step to step: it builds up along a valley floor, the back-and-forth zig-zags cancel, and small dips cannot stop it.

Plain descentHeavy ball (momentum)

Both start on the same slope, with the same step size.

Math glimpse

vk+1 = β vk − α∇f(xk),   xk+1 = xk + vk+1

On a narrow bowl, steps needed grow like √κ instead of κ. For κ = 10,000 that is about 100 times fewer steps.

Where it breaks

The heavy ball can overshoot, and its speed-up is proven only near the bottom of smooth bowls. Is there a speed limit for any method that only uses slopes?

Act II · 1983 · Yurii Nesterov

The speed limit, and how to reach it

Nemirovski and Yudin proved a speed limit for any method that only looks at slopes. Nesterov found a method that reaches it: look ahead to where momentum is carrying you, and measure the slope there instead.

Math glimpse

yk = xk + β(xk − xk−1),   xk+1 = yk − α∇f(yk)

Error falls like 1/k² instead of 1/k. To reach accuracy 10−6: about 1,000 steps instead of 1,000,000.

A seed planted · 1951 · Robbins & Monro

What if every slope you measure is noisy? They proved you can still reach the bottom if the steps shrink at the right rate. Nobody needed this for 60 years.

Error vs steps on a bowl with κ = 100 (log scale)
Where it breaks

All of this assumes the exact slope. When the function is an average over a billion data points, one exact slope costs a billion calculations.

The next question

By the 1980s optimization ran factories, airlines and power grids. But every one of those functions was written down by a person. Could a machine learn its own function from examples?

III1958 – 2009

Optimization meets learning

The question this act inherits

We can optimize any function we can write down. But what is the formula for "recognise a handwritten 7"? Nobody can write it. The answer: let the error on examples be the function.

Act III · The key insight

Learning means making your mistakes smaller

A model is a machine with knobs θ. Its error on the examples depends on where the knobs are set. That error is a landscape, and training is the hiker walking down it. The oldest case is least squares, used by Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809) to fit orbits, the same problem that motivated Cauchy.

Math glimpse · empirical risk minimization

minθ  L(θ) = (1/N) Σi ℓ( hθ(xi), yi )

hθ: the model · ℓ: how wrong one prediction is · N: number of examples

Where it breaks

Act III · 1958 · Cornell · Frank Rosenblatt

The perceptron: a machine that learns from mistakes

A machine wired to a 20 × 20 grid of light sensors. Show it a shape and let it guess yes or no. If it is wrong, nudge its weights toward the right answer. It learned to tell simple shapes apart.

Math glimpse

ŷ = sign(w · x + b);   if ŷ ≠ y:  w ← w + η y x

Novikoff (1962): if some line separates the classes with margin γ, the perceptron makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes.

The New York Times, July 1958

The Navy revealed "the embryo of an electronic computer" that it expects "will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

The hype was premature by decades. The core idea, learning by correcting errors, was right.

Where it breaks

Novikoff's guarantee begins with if a separating line exists. For some very simple patterns, none does.

The light is ON when exactly one switch is up. This rule is called XOR.

A perceptron can only draw one straight line: ON on one side, OFF on the other.

Press "Try another line" and count the red crosses.

Where it breaks

Two lines plus a neuron that combines them (a hidden layer) solve it. But Rosenblatt's rule cannot tell a hidden neuron how wrong it was. Funding collapsed: the first AI winter.

Act III · 1986 · Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams

Backpropagation: sharing the blame

Picks up from:

Send the error backward, layer by layer, so every connection learns its share of the blame and which way to move. Then take one gradient step for all of them together.

Math glimpse · the chain rule

∂L/∂w₁ = ∂L/∂a₃ · ∂a₃/∂a₂ · ∂a₂/∂a₁ · ∂a₁/∂w₁

Reusing the shared factors from the output backward gives every gradient for about the cost of two or three forward passes (reverse-mode differentiation, Linnainmaa 1970).

Where it breaks

Each sigmoid layer multiplies the blame by at most ¼. Through 10 layers: (¼)10 ≈ 10−6. Deep networks barely learned (the vanishing gradient, Hochreiter 1991). Small data and slow computers brought a second winter.

Act III · 1992 – 1995 · Vapnik, Cortes, Boser & Guyon

Support vector machines: learning with a guarantee

Picks up from: · Uses: and

Of all lines that separate two classes, choose the one with the widest empty street. The problem is convex, so the answer is unique and guaranteed. The KKT conditions from Act II show that only the points on the edge of the street matter: the support vectors. With the kernel trick, the street can curve.

Math glimpse

min ½‖w‖²  subject to  yi(w · xi + b) ≥ 1  for all i

Street width = 2/‖w‖. A convex quadratic program.

Where it breaks

Kernel methods compare every pair of examples: N² work. For 10 million images that is 1014 pairs. And the features had to be designed by hand.

Act III · 2006 – 2009 · The Netflix Prize

A million dollars for a smaller error

Netflix offered US$1 million to anyone who beat its rating predictions by 10%. The data: about 100 million ratings from 480,000 users on 17,770 films. The winning idea family: describe each user and each film by a short list of numbers (tastes), learned by descent. In 2009 "BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos" crossed 10%, submitting 20 minutes before an equally good rival.

Math glimpse · matrix factorization

r̂ui = pu · qi,   min Σ (rui − pu · qi)² + λ(‖pu‖² + ‖qi‖²)

Simon Funk (2006): update pu and qi after each single rating, not after all 100 million.

Where it breaks

Web-scale data grows from millions to billions of examples. Reading all of it before every step is no longer possible. Funk's one-rating-at-a-time trick becomes the way of the next era.

IV2010s → today

Optimization at planetary scale

The problems this act inherits

Billions of examples make every exact slope too expensive. Deep networks suffer vanishing gradients. And Nesterov's theory assumed exact slopes, which we no longer have.

Act IV · 2012 · Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton

The year deep learning woke up

Picks up from:

ImageNet: 1.2 million labelled photos in 1,000 categories. AlexNet cut the top-5 error from 26.2% (runner-up) to 15.3%. Three ingredients: a mountain of data, two gaming GPUs, and a simple activation, ReLU, that lets the blame flow back through deep stacks. It was trained with SGD and momentum.

Math glimpse

σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) ≤ ¼   vs   ReLU′(z) = 1 for z > 0

ReLU(z) = max(0, z). The gradient no longer shrinks by ¼ in every layer.

Size of the blame reaching each layer (10 layers)
Where it breaks

Deep networks now train, but each exact slope requires a pass over all 1.2 million images, and the next datasets are a thousand times bigger.

Act IV · 2011 – 2014

A step size for every direction

2011 · Duchi, Hazan & Singer

AdaGrad

G ← G + g²
θ ← θ − α g / √G

Knobs that rarely move get bigger steps.

Breaks: G only grows, so steps shrink toward zero and learning stalls.

→
2012 · Tieleman & Hinton

RMSProp

v ← ρv + (1−ρ)g²
θ ← θ − α g / √v

Forget old slopes with a moving average. First shared on a lecture slide.

Breaks: no momentum, so noisy slopes still jostle the path.

→
2014 · Kingma & Ba

Adam

m ← β₁m + (1−β₁)g
v ← β₂v + (1−β₂)g²
θ ← θ − α m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

Momentum (1964) + RMSProp (2012) + bias correction. The default in most deep learning code today.

Still open

Adam is everywhere, yet why it works so well on deep networks, and when plain SGD generalizes better, is not fully understood.

Act IV · Today

One step downhill, computed by thousands of GPUs

No single machine can hold a modern model's training run. Each GPU takes a different slice of the data and computes a slope. The slopes are averaged, and every GPU takes the same step. It is still mini-batch SGD with a very large spoon, repeated for weeks or months.

Math glimpse · data parallelism

g = (1/K) Σk=1K gk,   θ ← AdamStep(θ, g)

The average of K unbiased slopes is still unbiased, with K times less variance.

Where it breaks

Now the limits are physical: communication between machines, memory, and electricity. And the theory still cannot fully explain why descent works so well on these landscapes.

Epilogue · The baton is now yours

What nobody fully understands yet

Theory

Why does it work at all?

Deep networks have rugged, non-convex landscapes with billions of directions. Act I says descent should get stuck. In practice it finds excellent solutions.

Generalization

Why do noisy steps help?

Models trained with stochastic steps often perform better on new data. Explaining this "implicit regularization" is an active research area.

Energy

Can we descend more cheaply?

Training large models uses enormous computing power. Better optimizers directly save energy and money.

Society

Fair, robust and private

How do we optimize for accuracy while also staying fair across groups, robust to attacks, and private, as in federated learning across phones? These are constraints, as in Act II.
