Part 1 · Training data

To a computer, everything becomes numbers

A photo is a grid of tiny squares called pixels. Each square is stored as numbers that say how bright it is.

Photos → brightness of every square

Sound → how loud, thousands of times a second

Words → which words appear, and how often

Colour names → we will see how in Session II

So: before a machine can learn, every example is turned into numbers.

Click from photo to squares to numbers. This is a 14 by 14 grid; a phone photo has millions of squares. You don't need to remember pixels. Just remember: the machine never “sees” a mango, only numbers.

Part 2 · Features

Features: what to remember

  • A feature is one clue about an example.
  • Clues can be numbers or words from a list.
  • Good features change when the answer changes.
  • A machine can only use the clues we give it.

In one line: features are the clues; choose clues that actually change with the answer.

Think about your subject

What clues would you use to tell a strong essay from a weak one?

Length? Spelling? Paragraphs? Ideas? Which of these really change with quality?

Take two or three answers on the essay question. Point out that some clues are easy to count (length, spelling) but don't really measure quality. Choosing features is a human job, and teachers are good at it.

Regression answers: “How much?”

Picks a box

Ripe · Not ripe

Regression

Predicts a number on a scale

₹ 23 … ₹ 46.5 … ₹ 52

The second kind of supervised learning. Regression predicts a number that can take any value on a scale.

  • How many ₹ will this mango sell for?
  • How many mm of rain will fall tomorrow?
  • How many minutes until the school bus arrives?

The word “regression” sounds strange. Just remember: predicting a number.

If the answer is a label from a list, it's classification. If the answer is a number on a scale, it's regression. Same data can serve both, as we'll see in two slides.

Part 1 · Problem 4 · Outliers

One strange value can squash everything

An outlier is a value far away from all the others.

Kabir’s height says 1520 cm. That’s taller than a five-storey building. One wrong entry squashes every other bar.

Always ask first: is it a typo, or real but rare?

Typo → fix it.  Real → keep it. A truly fast runner is not a mistake.

Press the button to fix the typo and watch the chart recover. Warn against deleting every unusual value: a student who genuinely scores 100 is an outlier too, and real.

Part 1 · Problem 5 · Scaling

Who did better: Asha or Ravi?

880%Asha
test out of 10
6060%Ravi
test out of 100

In raw marks, Ravi looks far ahead. As percentages, Asha did better.

Machines get fooled the same way. A mango’s weight (180 to 390) looks far “bigger” than its softness (1 to 3), so weight shouts louder.

Scaling puts every feature on a similar range, just like converting marks to percentages.

Teachers do this all the time when they convert marks to percentages before comparing. Machines need the same help, or big-number features drown out small-number ones.

Part 2 · Feature engineering

The doctor’s trick: combine two clues into one

Both weigh 70 kg. Is their weight healthy?

  • Weight alone can’t say: it’s the same.
  • Height alone can’t say either.
  • Combined, as weight for your height, the answer becomes clear.

Doctors call this combined number BMI. A machine can learn far more from one good combined clue than from two separate ones.

No formula needed. The point: combining two clues can create a much better clue. Mangoes too: length and width together make “size”.

Taste before you serve

Before we trust a model, we test it on examples where we already know the answer, and see how often it is right.

This is called model evaluation.

Never trust a model nobody has tested.

Ask: would you trust an app that predicts which students need help if nobody had checked how often it is right? Evaluation is that check.

The wrong way: testing on the practice mangoes. Scores look wonderful but mean nothing, like giving students the exam paper as homework.

Accuracy is marks out of total

The machine predicted “ripe” or “not ripe” for the 20 hidden exam mangoes.

Accuracy = how many it got right, out of how many it tried.

Teachers already understand accuracy: it's the percentage score. 18 out of 20 is 90%. But as the next slide shows, a high percentage can still hide a serious problem.
